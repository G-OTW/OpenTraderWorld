# Checking a source

## When to use

Before leaning on a claim that matters — a number you are about to put in a report, a fact that
would change a decision, anything surprising.

## The questions

1. **Who is asserting it?** A regulator, the company itself, a journalist, an anonymous account,
   or an aggregator repeating one of those.
2. **Primary or secondary?** A filing, a print, an official release is primary. Coverage of it is
   secondary, and coverage of coverage is noise. Trace back as far as you can and say where you
   stopped.
3. **When?** The date of the claim, not the date you read it. Republished old news is a routine
   trap.
4. **Measured or estimated?** Reported revenue is measured. Expected revenue is a forecast wearing
   the same clothes. Analyst targets, projections, and "sources say" are all estimates.
5. **What is the incentive?** Someone who benefits from the claim being believed is not
   disqualified, but it belongs in the report.
6. **Is it independently confirmed?** By a source that did not get it from the first one.

## Pitfalls

- **Volume mistaken for confirmation.** Twenty outlets repeating one press release is one source.
- **Precision mistaken for accuracy.** "$4.237bn" from a model is not more reliable than "about
  $4bn" from the filing.
- **Authority by domain.** A well-designed site is not evidence.
- **Your own memory.** You do not know what happened after your training data ended, and you
  cannot verify a claim by recognising it. Fetch it or flag it.

## Verification

For each load-bearing claim: source named, date given, primary/secondary marked, and independent
confirmation stated as present or absent.

## Report shape

Inline attribution — "(Reuters, 12 Mar, citing the Q4 filing)". Where a claim rests on a single
weak source, say so beside the claim rather than in a footnote nobody reads.
